## Hook

A personal experiment in how small design and psychology changes can make creating a habit feel easier and more motivating.

## Persona

I was struggling to drink enough water.
My doctor told me to drink more.
I kept forgetting, and I was losing interest.
I found Habit Tracker on the App Store to stay motivated.
I opened it to create a habit and felt lost.

## Product problem

The habit creation flow made starting a habit harder instead of easier.
Clutter, too many choices, and little connection to my goal drained my motivation.

## Insight

Small points of friction can drain motivation at every step.
A habit is harder to start and keep when the flow feels empty, offers too many choices, demands too much setup, and ends without a moment of reward.

## Scope

Role: Product Designer (UX, UI)
Timeline: Jan 2024
Type: personal experiment
Methods: Journey Mapping, Design Psychology, UX Design, Competitive Analysis, Wireframing, Prototyping
Tested with: 5 people (informal)

## Beat 1: An empty start with no pull

The home screen felt empty, with little direction or energy.
A tiny plus button sat hidden in the corner.
It felt like I was expected to know what to do.

I simplified the first screen and removed extra icons.
Clear goals, progress, and rewards help people see why to begin.
I made the start button bigger and easier to reach.

📌 Goal visualization: Seeing a goal and progress can help people feel motivated to move forward.

📌 Fitts's Law: Larger buttons are easier and faster to tap.

## Beat 2: Too many choices, no clear start

After tapping Start New Habit, people saw a page full of icons, tabs, and categories.
It felt like a shopping list of habits.
It was hard to know where to start, what was active, or what the icons meant.

I restructured the New Habit page and added clear labels to each tab.
I limited the list to six simple starter habits.
I also proposed a social proof cue, with example text like “Many people stick with this habit.”

📌 Hick's Law: Too many options slow decisions. Fewer, clearer choices help people act faster.

📌 Social proof (proposed): Seeing others succeed can build trust and help people take the next step. This is a proposed cue, not measured data from my test.

## Beat 3: Too much to take in

After selecting a habit, the detail screen felt crowded.
Small buttons, clashing colors, and tightly packed fields made it hard to focus.
The form asked people to take in too much at once.

I split the long form into three lighter steps.
Each step gives people one clear thing to do.

1. Add Basics First: Start with only the essentials.
2. Asking for Commitment: Set a small target, with Daily and 18 days preselected.
3. Reinforce Progress: Encourage people to track progress and build consistency.

📌 Default Bias: People often stick with preset options. Daily and 18 days give them a helpful starting point.

## Beat 4: The missing celebration

People tapped Complete and got no feedback.
They went straight back to the homepage.
The achievement moment was lost.

A short “Congrats, you did it” moment recognizes their achievement.
It gives them a small high five and nudges their next step.

📌 Peak-End Rule: People remember the high point and the ending. A celebration makes the journey feel complete and helps bring them back.

## Honest outcomes

This was a personal experiment, tested informally with 5 people.
These observations come from those tests.

- People completed the flow 2x faster on average.
- Setup took under 1 minute, compared with about 3 minutes before.
- The steps felt clearer and had less friction.
- Small wins and feedback helped keep people engaged.

## Recap

An empty start and too many choices made it harder to begin.
A dense form added friction. A flat ending gave little reason to continue.
A meaningful start made the goal feel within reach.
Clearer choices and light steps made progress easier.
A small celebration gave the effort a satisfying finish.
