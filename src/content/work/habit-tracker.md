## Context

This was a personal experiment in January 2024. I was struggling with dehydration. My doctor told me to drink more water, and I kept forgetting.

I looked for an app to stay motivated and found one on the App Store called Habit Tracker. The moment I tried to create a habit, I felt lost. Too many options, too much clutter, and nothing that felt tied to my goal. I redesigned the habit creation flow to be simple, focused, and a little more delightful.

## Problem

There was no push to begin. The home screen was empty, with a tiny plus button in the corner, unrelated icons, and very little guidance. It felt like the app expected me to already know what to do.

The new habit page was full of icons, tabs, and categories. It looked busy and felt confusing. I was not sure where to start, what was active, or what the icons meant.

The detail screen was dense. Buttons were too small to tap comfortably. Colors clashed. Too many fields were packed together with poor spacing.

After I tapped complete, nothing happened. No feedback and no sense of finishing. The app dropped me back on the home screen, and the moment of achievement was gone.

## Process

I did not try to redesign the whole app. I fixed the moments where momentum died, one feeling at a time. For each choice I asked whether it would help someone take the next step.

I used journey mapping, design psychology, competitive analysis, wireframing, and prototyping.

On the home screen I removed extra icons and added clear goals. Each goal showed progress and a reward, so the start had a point. Seeing a goal makes it easier to move. I also made the main button larger and easier to reach. That follows Fitts's law. A larger target is easier to hit.

On the new habit page I labeled the tabs, so the category is obvious. I added a social proof line in the interface that reads "74% found success". It is a cue in the design, not a measured result of this test. I also limited the list to six starter habits. Hick's law says too many options slow a decision.

The long form became three steps.

1. Ask only for the basics, so the start stays light.
2. Ask for a small target. Daily and 18 days are preselected. People tend to keep a preset, which is default bias, so the preset should be a useful one.
3. Ask them to track progress, so the habit has a next visit.

At the end I added a short note: "Congrats! You did it!" It recognizes the finish and points to a next step. People remember the peak and the ending of an experience. That is the peak end rule.

## Results

I tested the redesigned flow with a few people.

- Less friction. Users completed the flow twice as fast on average.
- More clarity. The steps were simpler to follow.
- A motivation boost. Small wins and feedback kept them engaged.
- Higher completion. Users finished setup in under 1 minute. Previously it took about 3 minutes.

The lesson I kept: when a flow feels simple, people act because it feels natural. Good design makes people feel capable.
